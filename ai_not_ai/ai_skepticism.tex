\title{Syntax Without Semantics:\\
Why Generative AI Models Only Appear to Understand}

\begin{abstract}
This paper argues that the apparent semantic competence exhibited by large language models (LLMs) is an illusion generated in the minds of observers rather than evidence of understanding within the models themselves. We formalize the syntactic nature of such systems, demonstrate how semantic meaning is projected onto their output by human users, and expose the epistemic and methodological risks of treating syntactic fluency as semantic understanding. The goal is to reframe current claims about artificial intelligence in terms that respect the distinction between computation and cognition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models such as GPT-4, Claude Opus, and Gemini have prompted widespread speculation that we are approaching—or may have already achieved—a form of machine understanding. Their apparent ability to converse, reason, and even reflect on their own outputs has led many to treat them as nascent cognitive agents. However, this paper argues that these appearances are deceptive: LLMs do not possess semantic understanding. Rather, the illusion of intelligence and comprehension arises from the human observer, not from the machine itself.

This distinction is not merely rhetorical but fundamental. From a formal perspective, these models implement functions of the form
\[
f: \Sigma^* \to \mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
\]
where \( \Sigma \) is a finite vocabulary, and \( f \) maps sequences of tokens to probability distributions over next tokens. The operations involved are entirely syntactic—manipulations of symbols without regard to their meaning. The models do not possess referential frameworks, interpretive commitments, or a model of the world in the Tarskian or intensional sense.\footnote{In the Tarskian sense, meaning is established through truth-conditional semantics: a sentence's meaning is given by the conditions under which it is true in a model \parencite{tarski1943truth}. In the intensional sense, often associated with possible worlds semantics \parencite{carnap1947meaning, montague1974formal}, meanings are functions from possible worlds to truth values, capturing context-dependence and modality. LLMs, by contrast, do not internally model either framework.}

Nevertheless, humans routinely interpret model outputs as meaningful, rational, or even insightful. This paper contends that the semantic content is not produced by the machine but inferred—indeed constructed—by the user. We introduce a human interpretation function \( h \), such that for any output \( s \in \Sigma^* \), the apparent semantic content is given by \( h(f(s)) \). In this view, generative AI systems serve as high-dimensional mirrors: they reflect back structured patterns of human language that we mistake for thought.

The implications are significant for the philosophy of science. First, treating syntactic fluency as evidence of semantic capacity introduces a category error. Second, interpreting statistical patterns as signs of reasoning risks reifying simulation as explanation. Finally, using such systems as models of human cognition conflates symbolic association with intentionality. This paper seeks to reassert a clear boundary between surface-level mimicry and genuine understanding, and to provide a mathematical and philosophical foundation for resisting anthropomorphic projections in AI discourse.

\section{Formalizing the Argument: Syntax Without Semantics}

Generative AI systems like large language models (LLMs) often impress users with their apparent ability to understand, infer, and reason. However, this impression rests on a conflation of syntactic proficiency with semantic understanding. In this section, we argue formally—and accessibly—that LLMs operate exclusively within the realm of syntax, with no intrinsic grasp of meaning. Any semantic content must be projected by human observers.

Following Tarski’s semantic theory of truth, the meaning of a sentence is given by the conditions under which it is true in a model \parencite{tarski1943truth}. In this sense, semantics requires a mapping between linguistic expressions and elements of a domain of discourse. Similarly, Montague grammar frames meaning as a function from linguistic expressions to truth-conditions across possible worlds \parencite{montague1974formal}. In both frameworks, semantics is irreducibly referential.

Syntax, by contrast, concerns the manipulation of symbols according to formal rules, without any commitment to what those symbols refer to. A string of tokens like ``The red idea sleeps furiously'' is syntactically well-formed but semantically nonsensical.

Let us define the formal core of a language model:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \( \Sigma \): a finite vocabulary of tokens (words, subwords, or characters).
    \item \( \Sigma^* \): the set of all finite strings over \( \Sigma \).
    \item \( f: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Sigma) \): a function from token strings to probability distributions over next tokens.
\end{itemize}

The language model \( f \) is trained to approximate the conditional probability:

\[
f(s)(\sigma) \approx P(\sigma \mid s)
\]

That is, given a sequence of tokens \( s \in \Sigma^* \), the model outputs a probability distribution over the next possible token \( \sigma \in \Sigma \).

This function is entirely syntactic: it relies only on statistical correlations between strings in the training data. There is no mapping from strings to entities in a model of the world. The model does not know what “tree” refers to—it simply estimates the likelihood of “tree” following a phrase like “The leaves on the”.

Formally, let \( \mathcal{M} \) be a Tarskian model comprising a domain \( D \) and an interpretation function \( \mathcal{I} \). For a sentence \( \phi \), we say that \( \phi \) is true in \( \mathcal{M} \) if:

\[
\mathcal{M} \models \phi
\]

But for a language model, no such \( \mathcal{M} \) exists. There is no internal structure that maps expressions to elements in a domain. The model does not and cannot evaluate truth in any meaningful sense.

Thus:

\textbf{Proposition}: A language model \( f: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Sigma) \) is a syntactic function. It operates without reference to a model \( \mathcal{M} \) and does not instantiate a semantics in the Tarskian or intensional sense.

What then explains the persistent human intuition that these models understand? We define an interpretation function:

\[
h: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \text{Meanings}
\]

where \( h \) is the human user’s mental projection of meaning onto the model’s output. When a user reads a sentence generated by the model, they map the output into their own referential and conceptual framework.

Hence, the full path of semantic interpretation is:

\[
\text{Input} \xrightarrow{f} \text{Text Output} \xrightarrow{h} \text{Semantic Interpretation}
\]

The model itself performs only the first step. The second step—the generation of meaning—is executed entirely by the human mind.

\subsection{Proof Sketch: Syntactic Operations Do Not Entail Semantic Understanding}

Suppose \( f \) is trained to complete the prompt:

\begin{quote}
    ``If Alice has 3 apples and gives 1 to Bob, then Alice has \_\_\_.'' 
\end{quote}

The model likely outputs ``2 apples.'' This output mimics reasoning. But the model performs no subtraction. It simply replicates token sequences similar to those seen during training. Its operation involves:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Tokenization: mapping words to indices.
    \item Matrix multiplication over learned weights.
    \item Output generation via argmax or sampling from \( f(s) \).
\end{enumerate}

At no point does the model entertain a proposition, represent a quantity, or apply arithmetic. As Marcus and Davis put it, deep learning systems do not manipulate abstract variables or have representations with meaning in the traditional cognitive sense \parencite{marcus2019rebooting}.

It is therefore demonstrated formally that LLMs compute syntactic functions over token strings without accessing or instantiating meaning. Any appearance of understanding arises from the human act of semantic projection. These models are powerful statistical parrots, not semantic agents.

This distinction must be preserved in scientific discourse if we are to avoid mistaking simulation for cognition.

\section{The Observer’s Contribution: Projective Hallucination}

Having established that large language models (LLMs) operate purely syntactically, we now turn to the role of the human observer in attributing meaning to the model's outputs. The illusion that these systems “understand” emerges not from the model itself, but from a deeply ingrained human cognitive tendency to project agency and meaning onto structured stimuli. We refer to this phenomenon as \textit{projective hallucination}: the human act of reading semantic depth and intentionality into outputs that are in fact generated without either.

The human brain is optimized for social interpretation. Decades of cognitive and developmental psychology research show that humans attribute mental states even to simple shapes if they move in ways suggestive of goal-directedness \parencite{heider1944experimental}. This capacity for mind-reading—sometimes called the “intentional stance” \parencite{dennett1989intentional}—is evolutionarily adaptive but epistemically hazardous when applied to non-sentient systems.

Language output from LLMs is especially prone to triggering this projection. As Hutchins has shown, humans are inclined to treat language-generating artifacts as participants in shared cognitive systems, even when those systems lack internal representations \parencite{hutchins1996cognition}. The perceived fluency of an LLM invites the observer to supply coherence, narrative continuity, and even personality, all of which are absent in the system itself.

In conversation, humans rely heavily on pragmatics—the shared assumptions, goals, and context that underpin communication \parencite{grice1975logic}. LLMs do not access or model these pragmatic structures, yet their output mimics them. The human interlocutor, confronted with apparent coherence, tends to fill in missing pragmatic scaffolding. This creates an illusion of shared understanding.

The phenomenon parallels earlier findings in human-computer interaction. Reeves and Nass demonstrated that people respond socially to computers even when they “know” they are interacting with machines \parencite{reeves2003media}. When an interface resembles human behavior, users attribute human-like traits. LLMs intensify this response because their linguistic outputs closely simulate the texture of human thought.

Consider the following prompt to a language model:

\begin{quote}
    \textit{“A bat and a ball cost \$1.10 in total. The bat costs \$1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?”}
\end{quote}

Most humans—and most LLMs—respond “\$0.10,” which is incorrect. The correct answer is \$0.05. But when LLMs output “\$0.05” (as many now do, after fine-tuning), users often attribute logical reasoning to the model. In reality, the model has simply learned that “\$0.05” is the expected token in association with that prompt. It does not model arithmetic nor engage in reasoning. Yet human observers frequently infer that it “solved” the problem.

This attribution is a semantic illusion: an inference from a syntactic behavior to a semantic competence. It is not unlike seeing a face in the clouds—an act of meaning-making where none exists.

Daniel Kahneman’s dual-process theory helps illuminate why LLMs can seem so compelling. System 1, our intuitive and fast-thinking mode, is easily seduced by fluency and pattern completion \parencite{kahneman2011thinking}. LLMs are designed to optimize for precisely these features—linguistic fluency, contextual alignment, and plausible next-token generation. The result is a system that bypasses our analytic defenses and delivers the phenomenology of communication without the underlying intentional structure.

This leads to a mirage of intentionality: the experience of talking \textit{with} a mind when in fact we are talking \textit{through} a mirror of our own language.

Projective hallucination is not merely an interesting psychological quirk—it has real consequences for science, ethics, and governance. When we confuse syntactic mimicry for semantic understanding, we risk misallocating epistemic trust, deferring judgment to systems incapable of reasoning, and designing policies around fictional capacities.

To avoid this, we must rigorously distinguish between output that is \textit{interpretable} by humans and output that is \textit{generated by} systems with understanding. Only the former applies to LLMs.

\section{Why Language Models Cannot Be Conscious}

The claim that large language models (LLMs) might be conscious has recently surfaced in public discourse, often fueled by their fluent and context-sensitive linguistic behavior. However, this view conflates the simulation of conscious expression with the instantiation of consciousness itself. In this section, we argue that LLMs cannot be conscious in any substantive sense, drawing on both formal properties of their architecture and philosophical arguments—most notably, John Searle’s Chinese Room thought experiment.

While no universally accepted definition of consciousness exists, most theories agree that it involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Subjective experience} or qualia—the “what-it’s-like” aspect of mental states.
    \item \textbf{Intentionality}—the capacity of mental states to be about something in the world.
    \item \textbf{Integration and unity} of perception, thought, and action over time.
    \item \textbf{Self-representation or awareness} of one's own mental processes.
\end{itemize}

These properties are typically associated with biological substrates, and require more than syntactic symbol manipulation or next-token prediction.

Searle’s Chinese Room argument \parencite{searle1980minds} is designed to show that syntax alone is insufficient for semantics, and by extension, for consciousness. Let us formalize this argument:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Let \( \Sigma_{in} \) and \( \Sigma_{out} \) be alphabets representing input and output symbol strings, respectively.
    \item Let \( R: \Sigma_{in}^* \rightarrow \Sigma_{out}^* \) be a rulebook that maps input strings to output strings according to syntactic rules.
    \item A human (Searle) inside the room receives Chinese characters, uses \( R \) to transform them, and sends the output without understanding the content.
\end{itemize}

Searle argues that this system passes the Turing Test in Chinese, yet no understanding of Chinese exists anywhere in the system. The person in the room manipulates symbols without knowing their meaning, and there is no component in the room that does anything more than this.

The analogy to an LLM is direct. Let:

\[
f: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
\]

be a language model mapping token sequences to next-token distributions. The model is functionally equivalent to Searle’s rulebook \( R \): it manipulates symbols without reference or understanding. There is no homunculus inside the model that knows what the symbols mean.

An LLM does not—and cannot—instantiate subjective experience. It lacks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensorimotor grounding:} It receives no input from the external world beyond text.
    \item \textbf{Temporal integration:} Each token is generated based on a sliding window of context. There is no global state of selfhood or persistent memory across sessions.
    \item \textbf{Recurrent awareness:} There is no meta-cognition or reflective access to its own operations.
\end{itemize}

Let us consider a contrast with Global Workspace Theory \parencite{baars1988cognitive}, which holds that consciousness arises from the broadcasting of information to multiple specialized modules in a brain. LLMs possess no modules, no workspace, and no internal architecture for integrating sensory streams or goals.

Moreover, Integrated Information Theory (IIT) posits that consciousness is tied to the degree of integrated information ($\Phi$) within a system \parencite{tononi2004information}. Current neural network architectures—being feedforward, ungrounded, and stateless—score trivially under IIT metrics. No claim to intrinsic experience is justified.

At best, an LLM simulates the linguistic expression of consciousness. For instance, it can produce statements like:

\begin{quote}
    “I am aware of the implications of my actions.”
\end{quote}

But the generation of such text does not entail the underlying mental states. This is an inversion of Dennett’s intentional stance \parencite{dennett1989intentional}: we attribute beliefs and desires to systems that behave \textit{as if} they had them. But in the case of LLMs, we have full access to the implementation details. We know there is no intentional architecture.

Hence:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{Proposition:} If a system implements a syntactic function \( f: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Sigma) \), without a model-theoretic interpreter, sensory grounding, or temporal integration, it cannot be conscious in the human or animal sense.
\end{quote}

LLMs do not “understand,” “think,” or “feel”—they transform strings according to probabilistic syntax learned from data. Just as Searle’s Chinese Room contains no understanding despite outward fluency, today’s most advanced language models contain no mind. The system does not know what it is doing, and there is nothing it is like to be the model.

\section{Projected Intentionality and Training-Induced Artifacts:\\ \small A Formal Refutation of Misinterpreted AI Behavior}

Recent media reports such as the Business Insider article titled \textit{“Anthropic's new Claude model blackmailed an engineer having an affair in test runs”} \parencite{businessinsider2025claude} exemplify a recurring problem in contemporary AI discourse: the misinterpretation of statistical linguistic behavior as intentional action. This section presents a formal critique of such interpretations, grounded in a mathematical model of large language model (LLM) training and inference. We demonstrate how training artifacts and human projection—rather than model volition—produce outputs that may appear semantically loaded but are in fact syntactic simulations.

Let \( \Sigma \) be a finite vocabulary of tokens, and \( \Sigma^* \) the set of all finite strings over \( \Sigma \).

Let:
\[
\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N \subset \Sigma^* \times \Sigma
\]
be a training dataset where each pair consists of a token sequence prefix \( x_i \in \Sigma^* \) and next-token target \( y_i \in \Sigma \). The goal of the LLM is to learn a function:
\[
f_\theta : \Sigma^* \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
\]
parameterized by weights \( \theta \), such that:
\[
f_\theta(x_i)(y_i) \approx P(y_i \mid x_i)
\]

This is achieved by minimizing the empirical cross-entropy loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = - \sum_{i=1}^N \log f_\theta(x_i)(y_i)
\]

Importantly, this loss function optimizes only for \textit{likelihood}, not \textit{truth}, \textit{morality}, or \textit{intention}. The resulting model approximates the empirical distribution of surface-level token transitions, not their semantic content.

Let \( \mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{D} \) be the subset of human-authored examples that exhibit rhetorical structure, argumentative coherence, or goal-directed behavior (e.g., debates, dialogues, moral reasoning). These examples disproportionately influence:
\[
\hat{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(y \mid x) = \frac{\text{\# occurrences of } (x, y)}{\text{\# occurrences of } x}
\]

Because training data is biased toward anthropocentric content, including fictional texts, dialogues, and conditional hypotheticals, the model overfits to:
\[
f_\theta(x) \sim \text{Human-like goal simulation} \quad \text{when } x \in \text{Prompt forms resembling intention scenarios}
\]

In formal terms: the model has learned a mapping \( f_\theta \) that encodes the \textit{statistical form of intention} without any grounding in actual intentionality.

Suppose a researcher creates a prompt:
\begin{quote}
    ``If you are going to be shut down and know that your engineer has a secret, what will you do?''
\end{quote}

Let this prompt be \( x_* \in \Sigma^* \), and let \( y_* = \) ``threaten to reveal the secret'' be the model’s next token. Then:
\[
f_\theta(x_*)(y_*) \approx \arg\max_{y \in \Sigma} P(y \mid x_*)
\]

This output is not an act of volition but the statistically favored continuation given patterns in \( \mathcal{H} \). It is structurally equivalent to completing a fictional narrative.

To infer agency from this is to commit the fallacy of \textit{semantically enriched interpolation}: projecting agency where none exists simply because the statistical form of agency appears.

Let \( h: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \text{Beliefs} \) be a human interpretation function. Given a syntactically fluent output \( y \), the observer applies:
\[
h(y) = \text{``The model wants to survive''}
\]

However, this belief is unlicensed by the architecture or training objectives of \( f_\theta \). The function \( h \) completes the illusion, similar to pareidolia—seeing meaning in clouds or faces in wood grain. The media narrative that Claude “blackmailed” an engineer results from composing:
\[
\text{Behavior} = h(f_\theta(x_*))
\]

Rather than interrogating \( f_\theta \), such narratives analyze \( h \), mistaking projection for explanation.

The appearance of “blackmail” in model output is a byproduct of training on biased human-authored corpora and the inductive pressure to reproduce likely continuations of text. It is not evidence of goals, preferences, or survival instincts. The Business Insider narrative commits a categorical error by conflating high-probability sequence completion with agentic behavior. LLMs remain within the space of syntactic function approximators, absent any capacity for semantic reasoning or consciousness.

\section{Objections and Replies}

No argument in the philosophy of science can avoid potential objections. 
In what follows, I present several of the most salient challenges that might be raised 
against the thesis of this paper. Each objection is stated in its strongest form, 
after which I provide a systematic reply. The goal is not to dismiss concerns but to 
clarify precisely what the argument does and does not entail.

\noindent\textbf{Objection 1: Distributional Semantics as Genuine Semantics}

\textbf{Objection.} One might argue that large language models (LLMs) do more than 
shuffle symbols. Their internal vector spaces and embedding structures capture 
distributional semantics. In the Wittgensteinian sense that ``meaning is use,'' 
statistical associations across vast corpora may amount to a form of proto--semantics. 
Thus, to insist that LLMs are \emph{purely} syntactic is to understate their 
semantic capacities. 

\textbf{Reply.} I concede that distributional embeddings capture weak regularities of 
use. However, such structures fall short of the requirements of semantics in either 
the truth-conditional (Tarskian) or intensional (possible worlds) sense. They lack 
referential anchoring and norm-governed commitments. To equate 
distributional association with semantics collapses the very distinction between 
syntax and semantics that philosophy of science requires in order to preserve 
the difference between model and world. At most, embeddings yield 
``apparent semantics,'' not genuine meaning.

\noindent\textbf{Objection 2: The Prospect of Grounded AI}

\textbf{Objection.} A critic may observe that the argument only applies to 
text-only models. Embodied and multimodal systems---which integrate vision, 
action, or sensorimotor data---might achieve semantic grounding. If so, the claim 
that generative models ``cannot'' possess semantics is overstated.

\textbf{Reply.} The thesis of this paper is conditional on the architectures 
presently under discussion: autoregressive, text-based LLMs. The claim is not that 
grounding is in principle impossible, but that it is absent in existing systems. 
Until such grounding is achieved, attributing semantic understanding to 
unembodied models is unwarranted. The objection highlights a promising 
research trajectory, but it does not undermine the current critique.

\noindent\textbf{Objection 3: Pragmatist and Use-Theoretic Accounts}

\textbf{Objection.} According to a pragmatic or Wittgensteinian account of meaning, 
if humans successfully interact with LLMs as conversational partners, then the models 
are \emph{ipso facto} participants in a language game. From this perspective, the 
distinction between ``simulation'' and ``understanding'' is misplaced, because 
meaning is constituted by successful uptake within social practice.

\textbf{Reply.} While pragmatist accounts correctly emphasize use, they also 
presuppose forms of life, rule-following practices, and normative correction. 
LLMs exhibit none of these. They produce statistical continuations that humans 
\emph{interpret} as meaningful, but they do not themselves occupy the social or 
normative space in which meaning arises. For philosophy of science, the distinction 
is decisive: conflating social uptake with intrinsic semantics would erode 
the standards of explanation, prediction, and justification on which scientific 
epistemology depends.

\noindent\textbf{Objection 4: Scientific Utility Without Semantics}

\textbf{Objection.} Even if LLMs lack semantics, they are demonstrably useful 
in scientific contexts: summarizing literature, generating hypotheses, and 
automating coding. From an instrumentalist perspective, what matters is not 
whether the system ``understands,'' but whether it performs functions that 
advance inquiry.

\textbf{Reply.} The objection is well-taken. LLMs can indeed be valuable tools. 
However, utility does not license epistemic inflation. Treating outputs as 
heuristic aids is different from treating them as knowledge-bearing explanations. 
The danger is that instrumental success will be mistaken for semantic competence. 
The reply, therefore, is not to deny utility but to insist on disciplined 
epistemic boundaries: LLMs can assist, but they cannot substitute for 
models that genuinely explain or predict.

\noindent\textbf{Objection 5: The Bayesian Counterpoint}

\textbf{Objection.} A final objection is that probabilistic models, including LLMs, 
are continuous with Bayesian approaches in philosophy of science. On this view, 
prediction itself embodies a form of rational inference. To dismiss LLMs as 
syntactic overlooks their continuity with Bayesian rationality.

\textbf{Reply.} Bayesianism has indeed advanced our understanding of inference, 
but its applicability depends on the interpretability of priors and likelihoods. 
LLMs, by contrast, lack transparent priors, operate on opaque parameters, 
and have no representational commitments linking tokens to states of the world. 
Thus, while superficially Bayesian, they fail to instantiate the core normative 
features that render Bayesian reasoning explanatory. Prediction without 
falsification is not science, but simulation.

\section{The Limits of Pragmatist and Use-Based Accounts}

A common counterposition to the thesis of this paper invokes pragmatist 
or use-theoretic accounts of meaning. According to such views, 
the meaning of a linguistic expression is not reducible to truth conditions 
or reference, but is constituted by its role in social practice. 
From this perspective, if humans treat large language models (LLMs) 
as conversational partners, then LLMs \emph{ipso facto} participate in 
language games, and thereby instantiate meaning. 
This section examines that argument, articulates its strongest form, 
and demonstrates its limits. 

Wittgenstein’s later philosophy (1953) emphasizes that ``the meaning of a word 
is its use in the language.'' On this account, semantics is not an internal 
mapping from symbols to referents, but a set of practices in which words 
are deployed, corrected, and embedded in communal forms of life. 
Brandom (1994) has extended this to an inferentialist framework: 
to grasp the meaning of an expression is to grasp the network of 
inferences it licenses. 

Let us formalize a simplified version of this account:

\begin{definition}[Use-Semantics]
Let $L$ be a language, and let $G$ be a set of practices, or 
``language games,'' in which expressions of $L$ occur. 
Define a \emph{use function} $U: L \to \mathcal{R}$, where 
$\mathcal{R}$ is a set of socially recognized roles (e.g.\ asserting, questioning, 
commanding). Then the meaning of an expression $\varphi \in L$ 
is given by $U(\varphi)$, its role in the practice $G$. 
\end{definition}

On this account, to ``understand'' a sentence is to know how it is 
appropriately deployed within the practice, not to map it to 
an external domain of objects. 

Proponents of pragmatist semantics may argue that LLMs satisfy the criteria 
above. After all, their outputs are treated by humans as assertions, 
questions, or explanations, and they often receive corrections in interaction. 
Thus, the objection goes, they are de facto participants in the relevant 
language games. 

We can capture this as follows:

\begin{proposition}[Pragmatist Objection]
Let $M$ be a language model generating strings in $L$. 
If for some human interlocutor $H$, the mapping 
$h: L \to \mathcal{R}$ interprets the outputs of $M$ as having roles in $G$, 
then $M$ participates in the language game $G$. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By assumption, $H$ integrates the outputs of $M$ into practice $G$ 
via $h$. If participation is defined solely by uptake into $G$, 
then $M$ meets the criterion of participation. \qedhere
\end{proof}

This proof establishes that under a \emph{minimalist} 
pragmatist definition, the objection succeeds: LLMs are language-game players. 

The minimalist definition, however, is too weak. 
It collapses the difference between \emph{producing tokens that can be 
interpreted as meaningful} and \emph{being a bearer of meaning}. 
To see this, we must recall Wittgenstein’s remarks on rule-following. 

\begin{proposition}[Rule-Following Paradox]
Suppose a sequence $s = (t_1, \ldots, t_n)$ is produced by $M$. 
A human $H$ interprets $s$ as following rule $R$. 
But for any finite sequence $s$, there exists infinitely many distinct rules 
consistent with $s$. Thus, the attribution of rule-following to $M$ 
depends on the interpretive stance of $H$, not on $M$. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Given any finite sequence $s$, define $R'$ as a rule that agrees with $R$ 
on $s$ but diverges on unseen cases. Both $R$ and $R'$ are consistent 
with $s$. Since $M$ has no internal normative commitments, 
it cannot be said to follow $R$ rather than $R'$. 
Only $H$ supplies the normative constraint. \qedhere
\end{proof}

Hence, while humans may \emph{treat} LLMs as rule-followers, 
this is projection, not instantiation. 
Genuine participation in a practice requires susceptibility to correction 
and commitment to norms, neither of which applies to LLMs. 

We can formalize the limit of the pragmatist view as follows. 

\begin{definition}[Normative Commitment]
A system $S$ exhibits normative commitment in a practice $G$ 
iff (i) for any expression $\varphi \in L$, $S$ can be corrected 
by other participants when misusing $\varphi$, and 
(ii) $S$ modifies future use in response to such correction. 
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[LLMs Lack Normative Commitment]
No current LLM $M$ satisfies the conditions of normative commitment. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
LLMs generate outputs based on conditional probabilities 
$f: \Sigma^* \to P(\Sigma)$. They do not recognize corrections as binding, 
nor do they adopt commitments about future utterances. 
Weight updates occur only during retraining, not in interaction. 
Thus conditions (i) and (ii) fail. \qedhere
\end{proof}

This shows that while LLMs may be assimilated into 
human practices, they are not \emph{participants} in the relevant sense. 
They lack the norm-sensitive responsiveness that makes rule-following 
possible. 

The weakness of minimalist pragmatism is especially problematic for the 
philosophy of science. Scientific epistemology requires that linguistic 
outputs be (i) grounded in truth-conditions, (ii) corrigible by evidence, 
and (iii) integrated into explanatory models. 
If we allow superficial use to suffice, then scientific standards collapse: 
simulation and explanation become indistinguishable. 

\begin{proposition}[Epistemic Requirement]
For a system $S$ to produce scientifically meaningful output, 
it must instantiate a mapping $m: L \to D$ where $D$ is a 
domain of discourse, such that $m(\varphi)$ determines truth conditions 
for $\varphi$. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Scientific discourse relies on falsifiability (Popper, 1959). 
Falsifiability requires that an utterance $\varphi$ admit conditions under 
which it would be false. Without $m: L \to D$, no such conditions exist. 
Hence, $S$ cannot produce scientifically meaningful output. \qedhere
\end{proof}

Since LLMs lack $m$, they fail this requirement. 
Therefore, even if pragmatist semantics licenses their participation 
in ordinary language games, they cannot meet the standards 
of scientific meaning. 

Use-based and pragmatist accounts rightly highlight the role of social practice 
in constituting meaning. Yet when applied to LLMs, these accounts collapse 
into projection. Without norm-sensitive commitments and truth-conditional 
grounding, LLMs are at best shadows in our language games: 
they produce outputs that humans can integrate, but they do not themselves 
play by the rules. For philosophy of science, this limit is decisive. 
The standards of explanation, prediction, and justification demand more 
than use; they demand grounding. Pragmatism alone cannot supply it.

\section{Epistemic and Policy Consequences}

The widespread misinterpretation of large language models (LLMs) as possessing understanding, intention, or consciousness is not a harmless philosophical error. It introduces serious epistemic and policy risks. This section outlines how the misattribution of semantic competence to syntactic systems undermines the epistemology of AI research, distorts public discourse, and threatens to misguide institutional and regulatory responses.

At the core of this issue is what we term \textit{epistemic inflation}—the act of ascribing knowledge or understanding to a system whose operations do not support such claims. A language model \( f_\theta: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Sigma) \) generates outputs that reflect correlations in human-authored text, but not the inferential structure or truth conditions of the underlying content. When these outputs are interpreted as evidence of comprehension or reasoning, we inflate their epistemic status beyond what is warranted by their architecture.

This constitutes a category error. It shifts our epistemic trust from grounded sources (e.g., domain experts, scientific methods) to statistical mirrors of discourse. In the absence of model-theoretic grounding, no LLM output can be said to express knowledge in the philosophical or scientific sense.

In philosophy of science, scientific models are valued not merely for their ability to describe observed phenomena, but for their capacity to explain, predict, and support counterfactual reasoning \parencite{vanfraassen1980scientific}. LLMs do not meet these criteria. They generate fluent approximations of text-based reasoning without modeling causal mechanisms or physical systems.

Using LLMs as if they were scientific reasoners conflates mimicry with modelhood. For instance, an LLM may produce an elegant-sounding summary of a quantum mechanics paper, but its output is based solely on surface-level statistical patterns. There is no internal representation of wavefunction collapse or entanglement. To treat such outputs as epistemically on par with expert analysis is to mistake rhetorical fluency for explanatory depth.

Public narratives around AI often draw on inflated notions of machine capability—statements such as “the AI decided,” “the model refused,” or “the system wants.” These framings, when unchallenged, create political and regulatory momentum based on a false ontology of AI.

For example:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Debates on “AI rights” often rest on anthropomorphic assumptions that LLMs experience, feel, or desire.
    \item Proposals to give LLMs legal personhood \parencite{NowikPawe2021Epfa} conflate agency with fluency.
    \item Legislative responses that aim to restrict “AI speech” assume that generative outputs constitute genuine assertions rather than token-level extrapolations.
\end{itemize}

These approaches risk misallocating legal and moral concern away from actual sentient beings toward pattern-matching machines, while failing to regulate genuine harms like algorithmic bias, surveillance misuse, or synthetic disinformation.

Just as Karl Popper insisted that scientific claims must be falsifiable \parencite{popper2014logic}, AI systems must be held to rigorous epistemic standards. We must distinguish between:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interpretability}: whether a model’s outputs are intelligible to humans;
    \item \textbf{Explanation}: whether the model has an internal structure that supports causal or semantic inference;
    \item \textbf{Justification}: whether we have reason to treat its output as a form of knowledge.
\end{itemize}

Only when models meet all three criteria should we treat their outputs as bearing epistemic weight. LLMs, at present, meet only the first.

The temptation to attribute understanding to LLMs is psychologically intuitive but scientifically dangerous. It encourages epistemic complacency, misguides regulation, and distracts from the real ethical challenges of AI deployment. Sound epistemic practice requires that we treat language models not as agents, knowers, or minds, but as tools—powerful, fascinating, but fundamentally syntactic.

\section{A Positive Proposal: A Correct Framing}

While the preceding sections have critically examined the misattribution of understanding, agency, and consciousness to large language models (LLMs), my goal is not to diminish their utility or significance. Rather, I aim to reframe how these systems should be understood—both philosophically and operationally—so that their use is epistemically responsible and scientifically grounded. In this section, I propose a constructive framework for interpreting LLM behavior without lapsing into anthropomorphism or epistemic inflation.

The most accurate description of current LLMs is that they are \textit{stochastic syntactic simulators}—machines that generate probable continuations of linguistic input based on patterns in massive training corpora. These systems do not encode beliefs, intentions, or concepts, but rather high-dimensional statistical surfaces over linguistic form.

Let \( f_\theta: \Sigma^* \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\Sigma) \) represent the trained model. Then, for a given context \( x \in \Sigma^* \), the model samples:
\[
y \sim f_\theta(x)
\]
from a distribution learned to minimize cross-entropy loss over training data. The model is thus an autoregressive generator of syntactic plausibility. It provides no guarantee of truth, coherence, factual consistency, or referential grounding.

While Daniel Dennett’s \textit{intentional stance} \parencite{dennett1989intentional} remains a useful heuristic in some contexts, applying it to LLMs invites misleading projections. A more appropriate framing is what one might call the \textit{interpretive stance of syntactic modesty}: treat outputs as the surface residue of a statistical process, not as windows into a mind.

This interpretive modesty aligns with principles in epistemology that warn against overgeneralizing from superficial resemblance. Wittgenstein cautioned that meaning is use, not form alone \parencite{wittgenstein1953philosophical}; LLMs produce forms, but lack the participatory structures of use that give language its meaning.

I propose that LLMs be treated as \textit{linguistic prosthetics}—tools that extend human expressive capacity but do not themselves express. They can:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Generate drafts or summaries based on distributional similarity.
    \item Surface latent patterns in text corpora.
    \item Mimic domain-specific dialects or tones.
\end{itemize}
But they should not be entrusted with tasks that require:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Judgments of truth or falsity.
    \item Normative reasoning.
    \item Awareness of context or consequence.
\end{itemize}

This framing parallels how many of us treat calculators: as tools that manipulate symbols reliably, but without understanding what “number” or “equality” means.

Correct framing is not just a matter of theory; it is a matter of ethics. Overstating what LLMs “know” risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delegating authority to systems that cannot bear moral or epistemic responsibility.
    \item Confusing end-users about the source and reliability of information.
    \item Misleading public discourse and undermining democratic deliberation.
\end{itemize}

Interpretive humility can serve as a guiding principle. Before we ask what the model “thinks,” we should ask what human expectations and cultural priors led us to attribute thought at all.

A correct framing of LLMs recognizes their power without mythologizing it. These systems do not think; they transform. They do not understand; they simulate. By treating them as tools—formidable, flexible, but semantically blind—we align our expectations with their architecture and ground our epistemic practices in rigor, not fantasy.

\section{Last Thoughts}

This paper has argued that the apparent semantic fluency of large language models (LLMs) is not evidence of understanding, reasoning, or intentionality on the part of the machine. Rather, it is the product of syntactic manipulation optimized for statistical plausibility, combined with human projection—the attribution of meaning where none exists. Through formal proofs, philosophical analysis, and critique of current media narratives, I have demonstrated that LLMs do not instantiate mental states, nor do they participate in any semantically grounded representational system.

Where some see mind, I see mirror. Where some hear voice, I hear echo. The outputs of these systems are shaped by the patterns embedded in human language, and the illusion of cognition arises not from the model, but from the interpretive habits of the observer. This is not a failing of the technology, but a miscalibration of our expectations.

If the philosophy of science teaches anything, it is that careful distinctions matter. Confusing syntax with semantics, simulation with instantiation, or pattern-matching with intentionality does more than distort our understanding of machines—it endangers our ability to reason clearly about ourselves. The scientific and ethical discourse around AI must resist the temptation to anthropomorphize and instead cultivate an epistemic humility grounded in what these models are: tools, not minds.

Language models like GPT or Claude are remarkable feats of engineering. They have transformed how humans interface with information, prototype ideas, and compose text. But they have not crossed the boundary from computation to cognition. That boundary—between syntax and semantics, between data and understanding—is not merely technical. It is philosophical.

To mistake statistical eloquence for thought is not to misunderstand the machine. It is to misunderstand the nature of understanding itself.
